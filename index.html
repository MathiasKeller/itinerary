Wednesday 12 August · Evening

Do not be on a train at 20:18

A partial solar eclipse, not a solstice — and a deep one. The Moon covers 91–93% of the Sun over Switzerland, the greatest partial eclipse here since August 1999, with nothing comparable again in our lifetimes.

Eyes — no exceptions

You need certified eclipse glasses (ISO 12312-2), and you need to buy them now, because they sell out in the final week. Ordinary sunglasses are not sufficient — they pass far too much light. Never look through binoculars, a telescope or a camera without a proper solar filter fitted in front of the lens; the optics concentrate the light and the damage is instant.

Even at 93% covered, the remaining sliver of Sun is roughly 10,000 times brighter than a full Moon. A brief unprotected glance can permanently burn the retina, and there are no pain receptors there to warn you.

Why the 17:00 train doesn't work

It reaches Zürich at 19:15 — nine minutes before first contact, assuming everything runs to the second. Any delay leaves you moving, or underground at Zürich HB, through the start of the event. Stay west and travel after 21:00, or leave much earlier. The spots below are ordered best to easiest.

Checked: the quai is not the best spot

In Lausanne the maximum falls at 20:19 with 92.2% coverage and the Sun only 3.8° above the horizon. That is low enough that terrain decides everything.

Looking west-northwest from the Lausanne lakefront, the Jura ridge sits roughly 26 km away and stands about 2.9° high from lake level. The Sun clears it at maximum — but by less than a degree, and drops behind it within about ten minutes afterwards. Local press makes the same point about Geneva, where the Pâquis side of the rade is discouraged precisely because the Jura blocks it.

These are my own estimates from the terrain, so verify your exact spot with a terrain-aware eclipse simulator before committing.

Best — Mont-Pèlerin, above Vevey

Height helps twice over: you rise, and moving east puts more distance between you and the Jura, which flattens it. At 1,080 m and some 42 km from the ridge, the Jura drops to under 1° — effectively a free horizon. Funicular up from Vevey, about 50 minutes from Lausanne all in.

Two catches: the Tour Plein-Ciel viewing platform closes at 20:00, before maximum, so plan to stand on the open ground rather than up the tower — and check the last funicular down before you go up.

Mont-Pèlerin funicular Tour Plein-Ciel
Good compromise — Chexbres or the Lavaux terraces

Around 600 m and 35 km out, which puts the Jura near 1.8° — a clear improvement on lake level for very little effort. Twenty minutes by train, and you will already know the ground from the morning walk. Vines below you, open west, and somewhere to sit.

Chexbres
If you want company — Villeneuve

Local coverage names Villeneuve, at the head of the lake, as the standout spot in the region, sitting squarely on the sunset axis. The Vérossaz Observatory Foundation and the StarDome planetarium are running an eclipse festival there, so expect telescopes with proper filters, people who know what they are looking at, and crowds. Roughly 30 minutes by train.

Villeneuve
Easiest fallback — Plage de Vidy, not Ouchy

If you would rather not move far, Vidy is the Lausanne lakeshore spot the guides actually name, and it faces more openly west than Ouchy. Stand on the beach or the lawns right at the water; step back from the shore and the horizon closes up. Accept that you will lose the Sun behind the Jura shortly after maximum.

Plage de Vidy
Option B — be home early, then go up the Uetliberg

Swap the 17:00 for a departure around 15:20 and you're back by roughly 17:30 with time to spare. Then take the S10 up the Uetliberg — because from Zürich the eclipse ends at 20:42 when the Sun drops behind the Albis ridge, and the Uetliberg is that ridge. Standing on top of it is the difference between watching to the end and losing the Sun early.

The Urania observatory in the city centre also opens for events like this, but it will be extremely busy.

The one thing that can ruin it

At three degrees of elevation, a single band of low cloud in the west blocks everything, even under an otherwise perfectly clear sky. Check the western horizon forecast specifically, not the general one — and note that thin cloud or haze is not a safe filter. Glasses on regardless.

One consolation if it clouds over: the Perseids peak the same night, so stay out and look up anyway.
